2026 — now

nearbites

Co-founder · NUS Orbital, Apollo level

Open · shipped, tested with real users, pursuing commercialisation

A React Native app that redistributes surplus catered food at NUS. Organisers post leftovers with a GPS-captured location and a collection cutoff; students nearby get a push notification and a live map pin before the food goes cold.

React NativeExpoAppwriteGeolocationPush

The problem

Surplus food from campus events was announced across scattered Telegram groups. If you weren't in the right chat at the right minute, you never heard. Organisers had no way to reach people nearby, and students had no way to tell whether a post from forty minutes ago still meant anything.

My contribution

Two-person Orbital team. I covered the mobile app, the interactive map, the posting flow, preference-based onboarding and the backend integration.

What it does

  • Map and feed views, sortable by distance or by how soon the food expires.
  • Posting with GPS capture, manual pin placement and campus building lookup.
  • A radius each student sets themselves, from 50m to 5km, that governs their alerts.
  • Dietary and cutlery preferences captured at onboarding and used to filter listings.
  • Community updates so students can report whether food is still there.

What I learned

The hard problems weren't the features. They were location accuracy, listings going stale faster than anyone updates them, and requests that get interrupted halfway. An app like this lives or dies on whether people trust what it tells them, and trust is mostly a data-freshness problem wearing a UI costume.

Discover

Mobile interface

Expo Router screens covering feed, map, posting flow, profile and preferences, with state shared across them so a preference change reflows the feed immediately.

Connect

Appwrite backend

Authentication, structured listing documents, photo storage and real-time subscriptions, so a new post appears on other devices without a manual refresh.

Validate

Server-side rules

The service checks listing fields, location freshness and contribution limits rather than trusting the client, which matters the moment anyone has a reason to game the rewards.

Recover

Interrupted requests

Saved request identifiers and a transaction ledger let an interrupted submission resolve cleanly instead of double-posting a listing or double-awarding a reward.

The current codebase is a pilot. Notification tuning, account management and native-device acceptance work remain before a public release.

Radius is set by the student

A fixed alert radius would have been simpler. But someone in Kent Ridge halls and someone commuting in have completely different definitions of "nearby", and guessing wrong in either direction means either dead notifications or missed food. Handing over the slider costs one screen and removes the guess.

Community reports don't close a listing

When a student reports food as finished, we surface it as an unverified report rather than auto-closing. One mistaken tap shouldn't be able to hide real food from everyone else, and a wrong "finished" is more costly than a stale "available".

Cutoffs are labelled as declarations

The collection cutoff comes from the organiser, so the app says so in plain words. Presenting a guess as a fact is how an app stops being trusted the first time it's wrong.

Rewards are capped per day

Contribution points are limited per event and per day. Any reward system on a student campus will be tested, usually within a week, and it is far easier to cap at the start than to unwind inflated balances later.

Beijing housing market analysis

Data analysis · R and Tableau

Closed · academic project

Worked a large Beijing property transaction dataset into a recommendation on where to buy — testing whether subway proximity actually commands a premium, and which districts are defensible bets.

RTableauHypothesis testingForecasting

The question

Where in Beijing should a buyer put money, and does the conventional wisdom about subway proximity actually hold once you look at the transactions rather than the marketing?

What we found

  • Subway proximity commanded a premium in every district but one — and we reported the exception rather than trimming it.
  • The top five districts all showed positive forecast trajectories, with Chaoyang and Fengtai the most stable.
  • Haidian scored most evenly across spending power, transaction volume and community density, making it the least fragile choice.

My contribution

The statistical testing in R and the visual argument in Tableau. Every chart in the deck states one finding and its implication, so a reader doesn't have to reverse-engineer the point.

What I'd do differently

The t-test is on observational data, so it establishes association, not cause. Properties with those features may simply sit in better locations. With more time I'd control for district and building age before making any claim about the features themselves.

Define

Feature flag

Encoded a "winning" property profile in R — one bathroom, two living rooms, 60 to 89 square metres — as a binary variable to split the dataset on.

Test

Welch two-sample t-test

Compared buyer interest across the two groups without assuming equal variance. Roughly 2.57 more followers on average, with a confidence interval that stayed well clear of zero.

Report

Effect size, not just p

With a sample this large almost anything reaches significance, so the write-up leads with the size of the difference and the interval rather than the p-value.

Communicate

One claim per chart

Each Tableau slide carries an observation and an analysis line, so the finding and the "so what" travel together instead of relying on a presenter.

The role

The reappointment project

2021 — 2023

Cross-border sourcing

Sole operator

Closed · self-funded throughout

Built supplier relationships with dealers in Japan and Thailand and brought scarce streetwear into the Singapore market. Every unit was inventory I had already paid for, so the margin only existed if I had read demand correctly.

SourcingPricingNegotiationInventory

How it worked

Tracked release calendars and secondary-market pricing to find spreads before they closed, built relationships with dealers in Japan and Thailand for access to pieces that didn't reach Singapore, and sold into the local market.

What it actually taught me

  • Holding inventory you paid for is a completely different discipline from recommending what someone else should buy.
  • Relationships with suppliers compound. Price does not.
  • Most of the money is made in sourcing, not in selling.

Where I got it wrong

Early on I priced off what I thought a piece was worth rather than what the market had recently paid, and sat on stock for months as a result. It's the single most useful mistake I've made — it's the reason I now go looking for the number before forming the opinion.

Why it's here

Nobody assigned it, graded it or funded it. It is the cleanest evidence I have that I'd have started something anyway.

2021 — 2022

Weldy Wombats

Founder · Ngee Ann Polytechnic

Closed · handed over on graduation

Started a student volunteering organisation from nothing — name, identity and all — and ran programmes with Enabling Village and Extraordinary People.

FoundedPartnershipsVolunteer ops

Why

Volunteering at my polytechnic was mostly one-off drives with no continuity — good intentions, no relationship with the organisations on the other end. I wanted something that would still be running after the people who started it left.

What we ran

Built the organisation and its identity from zero, then established partnerships with Enabling Village and Extraordinary People, recruiting and coordinating student volunteers for art and engagement sessions with their communities.

What I learned

Getting volunteers to sign up is easy. Getting them to come back is the whole job, and it depends almost entirely on whether the first session felt useful rather than performative. We restructured sessions around that.

How it ended

Handed over to the next cohort on graduation. Building something that outlasts you turns out to be a different skill from building it in the first place.

2021 — 2022

New Balance promotional campaign

Team lead · top 4 of cohort

Closed · industry partner campaign

Led a team to diagnose a real brand problem for New Balance and pitch a rebrand around it, carried through customisable product, packaging and a pop-up concept.

StrategyPitchingBrandConcept design

The brief

An industry partner campaign: identify a real problem facing New Balance and answer it. We took the brand's own name as the problem worth interrogating.

The idea

"The balance" — approaching sustainability and gender equality as the same question of who a heritage sportswear brand is actually for, rather than as two separate campaigns bolted on.

The execution

  • Interchangeable velcro side panels, so one pair of shoes changes with the wearer instead of being replaced.
  • Packaging and a lockup built off the same mark.
  • A shipping-container pop-up styled as an oversized shoebox.

Result

Top four in the cohort. What I'd argue got us there was refusing to let the idea stay a slogan — every deliverable had to be a thing you could hold, not a line on a slide.
