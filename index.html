2026 — now

nearbites

Co-founder · NUS Orbital 2026, Apollo

Open · shipped and user-tested, working toward commercialisation

A React Native app for leftover catered food at NUS. Organisers post surplus with a GPS location, portion count, dietary tags and a best-before time. Students find it on a feed sorted by distance or expiry, or on a live campus map.

React NativeExpoAppwriteJestGitHub Actions

The problem

NUS student groups cater a lot of events, and a lot of that food is left over. Right now it gets posted in the NUS Buffet Response Telegram group, which carries so many messages that most members have muted or archived it.

The posts themselves aren't much better. They usually don't say whether the organiser actually approved the giveaway, where exactly the food is inside a building, or whether it's halal. So food gets binned while people who would have eaten it never saw the message.

What I built

  • Auth and a multi-step onboarding that collects dietary tags, food categories and a distance range.
  • A three-step posting flow: details, then logistics, then one to four photos.
  • A home feed you can sort by distance or by best-before time.
  • An interactive campus map with pins and a sliding detail sheet.
  • The Appwrite data layer behind all of it.

Where it stands

Apollo, Orbital's second-highest level. Auth, onboarding, posting, feed and map are all done and tested on device. Push notifications are not — the backend is built but I couldn't finish end-to-end testing. The Bugs tab explains why.

What I'd tell you if you asked

Almost none of the hard parts were the features. They were date strings that parse fine on one device and throw on another, photo uploads that lie about their own size, and GPS addresses that are technically correct and useless for actually finding the food.

Auth

One source of truth

Screens never talk to Appwrite directly. Auth calls go through a service layer and update a global UserContext, so every component reads the same user state and nobody re-fetches it. Expo Router route groups keep unauthenticated users out of the app routes.

Onboarding

Ask later, not at signup

Preferences are collected across several screens after registration rather than in one long form. Answers sit in React Context until the last step, so going back doesn't wipe them, and each step validates on its own.

Posting

Three steps, then upload

Details, then logistics (portions, best-before, GPS reverse-geocoded to an address, optional pickup note), then photos. Images go to Appwrite Storage first; the file IDs plus an ISO-8601 timestamp go into the listing document.

Feed and map

Sorting and stale listings

Distance uses a Haversine approximation. Pins are colour-coded green, orange or red off the best-before time, and posts more than an hour past expiry drop out of both the feed and the map.

Testing

Jest, and it runs itself

Unit tests on distance maths, pin colour logic, visibility filters, distance formatting and post validation. They run on every push and pull request through GitHub Actions.

Process

Branches and PRs

Component-based architecture, feature branches, changes reviewed through pull requests rather than pushed to main.

"12.00pm" crashed the app

JavaScript's Date constructor wouldn't parse time strings with a dot separator, and threw a RangeError on some environments but not others. Fixed by pulling the hour and minute out manually and building the Date directly instead of trusting the parser.

Photos too small to upload

I was passing photo.base64.length as the file size, which is the character count of the string, not the byte count. Appwrite Storage kept returning EntityTooSmall until I computed the actual byte size.

Push tokens with nowhere to go

Tokens were meant to save to a user's preferences on login, but for new users that document didn't exist yet, so the write silently did nothing.

The notification wall

A real ExponentPushToken only comes from a native build. Expo Go hands back its own shared token instead, so the save step was quietly skipped through every test session. iOS needed a US$99 developer account I couldn't justify. I built an Android APK through EAS, but token registration still failed on device.

The Appwrite Function, the database trigger, the preference matching and the Expo push API call are all written and verified in isolation. There was just never a recipient to send to.

Feed didn't refresh

Posting worked, but the new listing didn't appear — the home screen was already mounted, so nothing re-fetched.

Correct addresses, useless directions

Reverse geocoding gave a building name, which tells you nothing about which floor the food is on. This one came straight out of user testing.

How we tested

Five NUS students across different faculties used the app on their own, with no guidance from us. Each registered, onboarded, posted a food event, then tried to find listings on the feed and the map. We asked them about each stage afterwards.

What came back

  • All five got through onboarding and found listings.
  • Four of five found the three-step posting flow intuitive; one got stuck on GPS versus Building mode until they read the labels.
  • Three of five wanted the event name more prominent on the card.
  • One tester's map opened outside NUS.
  • No crashes. Four of five said they'd use it.

What we changed because of it

  • Added a pickup location description, since GPS coordinates alone couldn't get anyone to the right room.
  • Added building, floor and unit fields after testers found reverse-geocoded names too vague.
  • Made expired posts disappear from the feed automatically, because people kept seeing stale listings.

What I took from it

Every change above came from watching someone fail at something we thought was obvious. The most common request we didn't get to was search and filtering on the feed.

Beijing housing market analysis

BT2102 · six-person team · R and Tableau

Closed · academic project

A property firm wants to enter Beijing. We took a transaction dataset of several hundred thousand listings and answered where to buy, what to build, when to enter, and what the data doesn't support.

RTableaut-testForecastingData cleaning

The brief

Advise a property firm entering Beijing, across investment, development, leasing and mall management. Beijing's market swings hard — new-home prices rose about 30% year-on-year at the 2016 peak, then went negative by 2018. Four rounds of cooling measures have cut transaction volume repeatedly without pulling prices back down.

My section

[FILL THIS IN — which segment was yours? Investment, Development, Leasing or Mall Management. Name it and say what you personally produced.]

What we told them

  • Subway proximity commands a premium in every district but one.
  • Chaoyang is the premium market; Fangshan and Changping are the value plays.
  • April to June has the lowest median price and the lowest buyer interest — cheaper, with less competition.
  • Build 60–89 sqm, one bathroom, two living rooms, steel-concrete composite.
  • Redevelop Xicheng first: 69% of its stock predates 2000 and it scored highest on our opportunity index at 0.71.

What I'd caveat

The t-test runs on observational data, so it shows association, not cause. Properties with those features may just sit in better locations. I'd want to control for district and construction year before claiming the features themselves drive demand.

Clean

What we threw out

Dropped #NAME? errors and mojibake, values outside their valid range (0s in a field that only accepts 1–4), and rows with NaN or blanks.

Clean

What we kept but didn't use

Days-on-market was missing for roughly half the dataset. Dropping those rows would have skewed everything else, so we kept the rows and excluded the attribute from the analysis instead.

Scope

Urban districts only

The dataset covers urban districts, around 6,343 people per sq km against roughly 194 in the rural ones. We said so rather than implying city-wide coverage.

Test

Welch two-sample t-test

Flagged listings matching the winning feature profile in R and compared buyer interest across the two groups without assuming equal variance. About 2.57 more followers on average, confidence interval well clear of zero.

Score

Opportunity index

Normalised price, normalised followers and a feature-gap score, averaged. It finds districts that are valuable and in demand but still short on the product type buyers want. Xicheng topped it.

Present

One claim per chart

Every slide carries an observation and the decision that follows from it, so the finding travels with its "so what" instead of needing a presenter.

Three districts where elevators made properties cheaper

Elevators add a premium almost everywhere in Beijing. In three central districts the premium was negative, which looks like a data error.

It wasn't an error

Those three districts have a median construction year before 1997, when elevators weren't standard. Their housing stock is heritage low-rise hutong property, and that's what buyers are paying for. A building with an elevator in that area is a newer, less desirable one.

Why it mattered

It flipped a recommendation. Rather than chasing elevator premiums city-wide, we told the client to avoid central districts for that strategy and focus on outer ones, where the stock is newer and the premium is real.

The other one worth knowing

Refined decoration is a grade above Simplicity, but it had a lower median price. So renovating to Simplicity is enough — anything beyond it costs money without moving the sale price.

2021 — 2023

Cross-border sourcing

Sole operator

Closed · self-funded throughout

Built relationships with dealers in Japan and Thailand and brought scarce streetwear into the Singapore market. Every unit was stock I'd already paid for, so the margin only existed if I'd read demand right.

SourcingPricingNegotiationInventory

How it worked

I tracked release calendars and secondary-market prices to find spreads before they closed, then built relationships with dealers overseas for access to pieces that didn't reach Singapore at all. Sourcing, authentication, pricing and cash flow were all mine, with no outside capital.

What it taught me

  • Holding stock you paid for is nothing like recommending what someone else should buy.
  • Supplier relationships compound. Price doesn't.
  • Most of the money is made when you buy, not when you sell.

Where I got it wrong

Early on I priced off what I thought a piece was worth instead of what the market had recently paid, and sat on the stock for months. It's the most useful mistake I've made — it's why I now find the number before I form the opinion.

Why it's on here

Nobody assigned it, graded it or funded it. It's the clearest evidence I have that I'd have started something anyway.

2021 — 2022

New Balance campaign

Team lead · industry partner project · top 4 of cohort

Closed · Ngee Ann Polytechnic

New Balance had an indistinct brand image and weak pull with Gen Z in Singapore. We built a campaign called "the balance" that made gender equality the thing the brand stood for, and carried it through product, retail and media.

Brand strategyProduct conceptRetailPitching

The diagnosis

New Balance's problem wasn't awareness, it was that nobody could say what the brand was for. The 990 line had been redesigned repeatedly since 1982 with no signature silhouette to anchor it. And in Singapore the 565 is the heavily subsidised shoe handed to every National Serviceman, which had quietly made the brand read as a cheap, disposable beater.

The idea

Take the name literally. "The balance" — an N X B mark built as a cross — with gender equality as the cause the brand advocates for. Not a one-off campaign but a position, built on the 550 line, which already had the Gen Z traction the 990 didn't.

How we made it real

  • Interchangeable velcro N-panels, ten designs, so one pair changes instead of being replaced.
  • A three-week Orchard Road pop-up in a shipping container styled as an oversized shoebox, with a VR station to try your own velcro design on the 550s.
  • A card wall at the entrance: write what you want to change about gender equality, and one card equals one dollar donated to the Singapore Women's Association. You leave with a tote made from upcycled material.

Result

Top four in the cohort. The thing that got us there, I think, was refusing to let the idea stay a slogan. Every deliverable had to be something a customer could hold or do.
