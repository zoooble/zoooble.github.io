2026 — now

nearbites

Co-founder · NUS Orbital 2026, Apollo

Open · shipped and user-tested, working toward commercialisation

A React Native app for the leftover catered food at NUS events. Organisers post what's left with a GPS location, a portion count, dietary tags and a best-before time, and students find it either on a feed they can sort by distance or expiry, or on a live campus map.

React NativeExpoAppwriteJestGitHub Actions

The problem

NUS student groups cater a lot of events, and a good deal of that food goes uneaten. At the moment the way people hear about it is the NUS Buffet Response Telegram group, which carries so much traffic that most of its members have muted or archived it, so the messages that would have mattered to them get buried with everything else.

The posts themselves also tend to leave out the things you actually need. They often don't say whether the organiser approved the giveaway, which floor of the building the food is on, or whether it's halal. So the food ends up in a bin while the people who would happily have eaten it never saw the message.

What I built

We were a two-person Orbital team. My side covered authentication and the multi-step onboarding that collects dietary tags, food categories and a distance range, the three-step posting flow, the home feed, the interactive campus map, and the Appwrite data layer that sits behind all of it.

Where it stands

We attained Apollo, which is Orbital's second-highest level. Authentication, onboarding, posting, the feed and the map are all finished and tested on device. Push notifications are not, although the backend for them is written — the Bugs tab explains what stopped us, because the reason is more interesting than the feature.

What I'd tell you if you asked

Almost none of the difficulty was in the features themselves. It was in date strings that parse correctly on one device and throw an error on another, photo uploads that misreport their own size, and GPS addresses that are technically accurate and still useless for finding food inside a building.

Auth

One source of truth

No screen talks to Appwrite directly. Authentication calls go through a service layer that updates a global UserContext, so every component reads the same user state instead of re-fetching it, and swapping the auth provider later would only touch one layer. Expo Router route groups keep unauthenticated users out of the app routes.

Onboarding

Ask later, not at signup

Preferences are collected across several screens after registration rather than in one long form, because asking for everything at once is how you lose people at the door. Answers sit in React Context until the final step, so going back doesn't wipe them, and each step validates on its own.

Posting

Three steps, then upload

Details first, then logistics — portions, a best-before time, GPS coordinates reverse-geocoded into an address, and an optional pickup note — and finally one to four photos. The images go to Appwrite Storage before anything else, and the returned file IDs are stored on the listing document along with an ISO-8601 timestamp.

Feed and map

Sorting, and getting rid of stale posts

Distance is calculated with a Haversine approximation. Map pins are colour-coded green, orange or red off the best-before time, and anything more than an hour past expiry drops out of both the feed and the map so nobody walks across campus for food that's gone.

Testing

Jest, running on its own

Unit tests cover the distance calculation, the pin colour logic, the visibility filter, distance formatting and post validation across both location modes. They run automatically on every push and pull request through GitHub Actions.

Process

Branches and pull requests

Component-based architecture, feature branches, and changes going through pull requests rather than straight onto main.

"12.00pm" crashed the app

JavaScript's Date constructor wouldn't parse a time string with a dot separator, and it threw a RangeError in some environments while working fine in others, which made it slow to pin down. I ended up extracting the hour and minute manually and constructing the Date directly rather than trusting the parser.

Photos that were too small to upload

I was passing photo.base64.length as the file size, which is the character count of the encoded string rather than the byte count of the file. Appwrite Storage kept returning EntityTooSmall until I worked out the actual byte size.

Push tokens with nowhere to go

Tokens were supposed to save to a user's preferences document on login, but for a new user that document doesn't exist yet at that point, so the write quietly did nothing and gave no error.

The wall we didn't get past

A real ExponentPushToken can only be generated from a native build. Expo Go, which is what we developed in, hands back its own shared token instead, so the save step was silently skipped through every testing session we ran. Testing properly on iOS needs a US$99 Apple Developer account, which we couldn't justify, so I built an Android APK through EAS instead — and token registration still failed on the device.

The Appwrite Function, the database trigger, the preference-matching logic and the Expo push API call are all written and verified in isolation. There was never a valid recipient to send anything to.

The feed that wouldn't refresh

Posting worked, but the new listing didn't show up, because the home screen was already mounted and nothing triggered a re-fetch.

Correct addresses, useless directions

Reverse geocoding gives you a building name, which tells you nothing about which floor or room the food is in. We only found this out by watching people try to use it.

How we tested it

Five NUS students from different faculties used the app on their own without any guidance from us. Each of them registered, went through onboarding, posted a food event, and then tried to find listings on the feed and the map, and we asked them about each stage afterwards.

What came back

  • All five completed onboarding and found active listings.
  • Four of five found the three-step posting flow intuitive; one was confused by GPS versus Building mode until they read the labels.
  • Three of five wanted the event name more prominent on the listing card.
  • One tester's map loaded outside the NUS bounds.
  • Nobody reported a crash, and four of five said they'd use it if it were available.

What we changed as a result

  • Added a pickup location description, because GPS coordinates on their own couldn't get anyone to the right room.
  • Added building, floor and unit fields after testers found the reverse-geocoded names too vague to act on.
  • Made expired posts disappear from the feed automatically, since people kept encountering stale listings.

What I took from it

Every one of those changes came from watching somebody fail at something we had assumed was obvious. The most common request we didn't get to was search and filtering on the feed, which is near the top of the list if we keep going.

ML Research Agent

TechJam 2026 · five-person team

Closed · hackathon prototype

An LLM-guided loop that proposes changes to a recommendation model, runs the experiment, reads the result, and decides whether to keep the change — then feeds that history back into the next proposal. Built against the KuaiRand recommendation benchmark.

PythonNumPyGemini APISubprocess

The problem

Machine learning experimentation is a repeated cycle: you propose a change, train, evaluate, then decide whether to keep it and propose the next thing. Most of that loop is mechanical, and the interesting part — deciding what to try next given everything you've already tried — is the part that gets rushed because the rest takes so long. We wanted to see how much of that cycle could be handed to an LLM on a real benchmark.

How the loop works

  • The model receives the current pipeline and the history of previous experiments, and returns a hypothesis along with a complete candidate pipeline.
  • The candidate's Python is syntax-checked before anything runs.
  • It executes in its own working directory as a subprocess, with output captured and a timeout bounding it.
  • The loop reads the output metrics and validates them against the structure the benchmark expects.
  • It then keeps or rejects the candidate and writes the score, the code difference, the decision and any failure into the history for the next round.

What I took from it

An experiment that crashes or times out still produces information, as long as the failure is captured in a structure the loop can read. Getting that right mattered more than getting more candidates to succeed, because a run that fails silently costs you the whole iteration.

We didn't place

It was a hackathon and we didn't win anything. I'm including it because the problem was more interesting than the result, and because it's the first thing I've built where an LLM was doing something other than answering questions.

It isn't a sandbox

A separate working directory and a timeout give you execution controls, not isolation. Running model-generated code safely is a harder problem than we solved in a weekend, and I'd want to say that plainly rather than imply otherwise.

Beijing housing market analysis

BT2102 · six-person team · R and Tableau

Closed · academic project

A property firm wants to enter Beijing. We took a transaction dataset of several hundred thousand listings and worked out where they should buy, what they should build, when to enter, and which of the obvious conclusions the data doesn't actually support.

RTableaut-testForecastingData cleaning

The brief

We were advising a property firm entering Beijing across investment, development, leasing and mall management. It's a difficult market to time — new-home prices rose around 30% year-on-year at the 2016 peak and had turned negative by 2018, and four rounds of cooling measures have repeatedly cut transaction volume without ever bringing prices back down.

My section

[FILL THIS IN — which segment was yours? Investment, Development, Leasing or Mall Management. Name it and say what you personally produced.]

What we recommended

  • Subway proximity commands a price premium in every district but one.
  • Chaoyang is the premium market, while Fangshan and Changping are where the value sits.
  • April to June has both the lowest median price and the lowest buyer interest, so it's cheaper and less competitive at the same time.
  • Build 60–89 sqm, one bathroom, two living rooms, steel-concrete composite.
  • Redevelop Xicheng first, since 69% of its stock predates 2000 and it scored highest on our opportunity index at 0.71.

What I'd caveat

The t-test is run on observational data, so it establishes an association rather than a cause. Properties with those features may simply sit in better locations, and I'd want to control for district and construction year before claiming the features themselves are what drives demand.

Clean

What we removed

Error values like #NAME? and mojibake, entries falling outside their valid range such as a 0 in a field that only accepts 1 to 4, and rows carrying NaN or blanks.

Clean

What we kept but didn't use

Days-on-market was missing for roughly half the dataset. Dropping those rows would have distorted everything else we were measuring, so we kept the rows and excluded that attribute from the analysis instead, and said so rather than leaving it implied.

Scope

Urban districts only

The dataset covers urban districts, at roughly 6,343 people per square kilometre against about 194 in the rural ones, so we were careful not to present the findings as city-wide.

Test

Welch two-sample t-test

We flagged listings matching the winning feature profile in R and compared buyer interest between the two groups without assuming equal variance. The difference came out at around 2.57 more followers on average, with a confidence interval well clear of zero.

Score

Opportunity index

Normalised price, normalised followers and a feature-gap score, averaged together, so that a district scores highly only when it is valuable, in demand, and still short on the product type buyers want. Xicheng came out on top.

Present

One claim per chart

Every slide carries an observation and the decision that follows from it, so the finding travels with its implication instead of relying on whoever happens to be presenting.

Three districts where elevators made properties cheaper

Elevators carry a price premium almost everywhere in Beijing, so when three central districts came back with a negative premium, the obvious assumption was that we'd made an error somewhere in the pipeline.

It turned out not to be an error

All three have a median construction year before 1997, which is before elevators were standard there. The housing stock in those districts is heritage low-rise hutong property, and that's what buyers are paying the premium for, so a building with an elevator in that area is likely to be a newer and less desirable one.

Why it changed the recommendation

Instead of telling the client to chase elevator premiums across the city, we told them to avoid the central districts for that particular strategy and concentrate on the outer ones, where the stock is newer and the premium is real.

The other finding worth knowing

Refined decoration is a grade above Simplicity, but properties with it had a lower median price. Renovating to Simplicity is therefore sufficient, and anything spent beyond that is money that doesn't come back in the sale price.

2021 — 2023

Cross-border sourcing

Sole operator

Closed · self-funded throughout

Travelling to Japan often, I noticed that pieces sitting on the shelf there were selling at a considerable markup in Singapore. I built relationships with the shops I kept returning to, later expanded into Thailand, and ran the whole thing on my own money.

SourcingPricingNegotiationInventory

How it started

I was travelling to Japan frequently enough to notice that certain pieces were still sitting on shelves there while the same things were selling at a significant markup back home. Because I speak conversational Japanese, it wasn't difficult to build a relationship with the staff at the shops I kept returning to, and over time they would tell me when something was due in rather than leaving me to find out on the day. That eventually expanded to a few contacts in Thailand as well.

What running it involved

I tracked release calendars and secondary-market prices to find the spread before it closed, handled sourcing and authentication myself, set the prices, and managed the cash flow without any outside capital. Everything I bought was stock I had already paid for, so if I misread what people wanted, the loss was mine to absorb.

Where I got it wrong

Early on I priced off what I thought a piece was worth instead of what the market had recently paid for it, and ended up sitting on that stock for months. It's probably the most useful mistake I've made, because it's the reason I now go and find the number before I form an opinion about it.

What it taught me

Holding inventory you've paid for is a different discipline from recommending what somebody else should buy. Supplier relationships compound over time in a way that price advantages don't, and most of the margin is decided when you buy rather than when you sell.

2021 — 2022

New Balance campaign

Team lead · industry partner project · top 4 of cohort

Closed · Ngee Ann Polytechnic

New Balance had an indistinct brand image and very little pull with Gen Z in Singapore. We built a campaign called "the balance" that gave the brand a position to hold, and then carried that position through product, retail and media rather than leaving it on a slide.

Brand strategyProduct conceptRetailPitching

The diagnosis

New Balance's problem in Singapore wasn't awareness, it was that nobody could say what the brand was for. The 990 line had been redesigned repeatedly since 1982 without ever settling on a signature silhouette people could attach to the brand, so there was nothing for a customer to recognise it by.

On top of that, the 565 is the heavily subsidised shoe issued to National Servicemen here, which had quietly positioned New Balance in most young Singaporeans' minds as a beater — something cheap that you wear until it falls apart. That's a local problem that wouldn't show up in any global brand study.

The idea

We took the name literally and built the campaign around "the balance", using an N X B mark set as a cross, with gender equality as the cause the brand would advocate for. It was framed as a position the brand holds rather than a campaign that ends, and we built it on the 550 line because that silhouette already had the Gen Z recognition the 990 didn't.

Why that cause

New Balance already had sustainability commitments in place through its Green Leaf line and its 2025 material targets, so sustainability wouldn't have differentiated them from anyone else in the category. Gender equality was a position none of their competitors were occupying locally, and pairing it with a donation to the Singapore Women's Association gave it something concrete behind it rather than a statement.

Result

We placed in the top four of the cohort. The thing that I think got us there was refusing to let the idea stay a slogan — we made every deliverable something a customer could either hold or do.

Product

Interchangeable velcro panels

The N on the side of the 550 becomes a velcro panel, with ten designs so one pair changes with the wearer instead of being replaced. The N X B mark sits on the heel, in a red and ivory colourway.

Retail

A shoebox on Orchard Road

A three-week pop-up in a shipping container styled as an oversized shoebox, open 11am to 10pm, with the 550s displayed in the centre and the ten velcro designs in glass cases alongside the story of how each came about.

Retail

Design your own, in VR

Visitors design a personalised velcro panel, scan it into a VR station to see it on the 550s, then submit it to an online public vote, with vouchers for the people who vote. It turns a browse into a reason to come back to the website.

Retail

The card wall

Write what you want changed about gender equality on a card and paste it to the display stand, and New Balance donates a dollar for every card. You leave with an upcycled tote, which then does the advertising for you.

Partner

Nail wraps with NODSPARK

A co-branded nail wrap kit and in-booth service, which extends the campaign to a product that isn't footwear and reaches an audience the shoe alone wouldn't.

Media

Commercial and social

Two storyboarded scenarios — a woman dismissed in a meeting until a colleague backs her, and a child meeting a Santa who turns out to be a woman — plus behind-the-scenes content on the upcycling process as a soft launch for the 550s.

The campaign was structured across owned, earned, paid and converged media, with each element given its own objective rather than being treated as general awareness.
